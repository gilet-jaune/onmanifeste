# Laws and freedoms: what changes for protesting

The texts governing demonstrations and security are not new: a declaration regime in 1935, the state of emergency in 1955, the "anti-rioters" law in 1970, counter-terrorism legislation since 1986. Since 2015, a series of laws, plans and trials has widened the powers of control, prevention and punishment around demonstrations: the state of emergency, the 2019 "anti-rioters" law, cameras with algorithmic processing, the "casseurs-payeurs" ("rioters pay") bill. Their supporters point to the fight against terrorism and violence; their critics (the UN, the Council of Europe, the CNCDH, the Defender of Rights, the LDH) see a rollback of freedoms. This page does not take sides: it dates each text, says what it changes and reports what the Constitutional Council and these bodies have said about it. Whether a law is "freedom-killing" is a political judgement: we cite who says so rather than asserting it.

!!! abstract "Key points"

    - **Every measure is dated and sourced.** We separate what the law says, what the court upheld or struck down, and what observers criticise.
    - **The Constitutional Council struck down** the administrative ban on demonstrating (2019), provisions of the "global security" law (2021) and a first extension of algorithmic video surveillance (2025), but **upheld** searches around protests, the face-covering offence and the trial of these cameras.
    - **In practice**, this affects searches, faces, bans on demonstrating, financial liability, cameras and the use of force: the matching practical guides are linked below.

## Not new: earlier precedents

Every era has had its texts; what has changed since 2015 is mostly the pace and the number. The precedents (the 1935 prior declaration, the 1955 state of emergency, the 1970 'anti-rioters' law repealed in 1981, counter-terrorism law from 1986 to 2006, and the 2016 ban on marches for lack of resources) are dated and sourced in the [full timeline](../histoire/index.md#kind=securite-libertes), where each has its own card.

## What these texts change, in practice

- **Searches and checks nearby.** Since 2019, on the prosecutor's written order, officers may inspect and search bags and check vehicles around a demonstration; the Constitutional Council upheld this provision.[^cc-2019-780][^wiki-loi-anticasseurs-2019] See [ID checks, bag searches and fines](../guides/controle-fouille.md).
- **Your face.** Deliberately covering one's face without legitimate reason, around a demonstration where disorder occurs or is likely, is punishable by one year in prison and a €15,000 fine. For the Constitutional Council, the text targets the intent to prevent identification, and covering for a legitimate reason falls outside the offence.[^cc-2019-780] See [Protest kit and outfit](../guides/kit-manif.md) (protecting yourself from gas).
- **Being banned from demonstrating.** The individual administrative ban was struck down in 2019; a ban ordered by a judge (additional penalty, judicial supervision) remains possible, for up to three years, and breaching it is punishable by one year in prison and a €15,000 fine;[^legifrance-loi-2019-290] these bans are entered in the wanted-persons file.[^wiki-loi-anticasseurs-2019][^cc-2019-780] Under the state of emergency (2015-2017), the authorities could ban gatherings likely to provoke or sustain disorder, in particular demonstrations;[^wiki-etat-urgence-france] the 2017 SILT law ended that ban on demonstrating, while keeping other state-of-emergency measures (protection perimeters, house orders).[^vie-publique-chronologie-antiterroriste] See [The right to protest](../guides/droit-de-manifester.md).
- **Paying for damage.** Since 2019, the State can take action against those who cause damage during unlawful gatherings, even without a criminal conviction (the "rioter pays" principle).[^legifrance-loi-2019-290][^landot-casseur-payeur-2026] The bill examined by the Senate in October 2026 adds joint liability (capped at €10,000) for anyone who knowingly took part in a violent group, or who kept taking part in an unlawful gathering after warnings while knowing violence was being committed, unless for a legitimate reason; it also provides for seizing social benefits to recover damages.[^senat-pjl-dommages-espace-public-2026] The Interior Minister sees it as a way to deter rioters and better compensate victims; the left and unions denounce it as an attack on the right to demonstrate.[^publicsenat-casseurs-payeurs-2026] See [Dispersal, warnings and kettling](../guides/dispersion.md).
- **Cameras.** Since 2023, a trial has allowed algorithmic processing of images from video protection or from cameras mounted on aircraft to detect and report certain events. It was extended until 2027 and then, by the Ripost law, until 31 December 2030 and to other places open to the public; that law also creates an urgent procedure for using cameras on drones.[^cc-2023-850][^cc-2026-902][^lcp-loi-jo-2030-2026][^cc-2026-915] Amnesty International France sees a normalisation of surveillance technologies.[^amnesty-vsa-prolongation-2026] The same "casseurs-payeurs" bill, in the Senate committee's text, would also authorise cameras on aircraft, on the prosecutor's order, around demonstrations to prosecute offences.[^senat-pjl-dommages-espace-public-2026] See [Film the police](../guides/filmer.md).
- **Force and weapons.** The Defender of Rights recommends banning the LBD in policing protests; the 2020 scheme keeps it with a supervisor alongside shooters, and in 2021 the Council of State struck down the possibility of encircling demonstrators for lack of precise conditions.[^ddd-avis-20-08][^ce-snmo-actualite] Article L. 435-1 of the Internal Security Code (2017), which lists the cases in which police may use their weapons, is contested by the LDH.[^ldh-armes-forces-ordre] In 2026, a bill creating a presumption of self-defence for law enforcement was passed by the National Assembly (7 July) and sent to the Senate; the Defender of Rights warns of its risks to the right to life and access to a judge, and a UN special rapporteur asks that it be rejected.[^an-ppl-presomption-legitime-defense][^ddd-avis-26-05-presomption][^ohchr-france-presomption-tirs-2026] See [Police weapons](../guides/armes-police.md).
- **Armoured vehicles and sound.** In December 2018, the gendarmerie deployed about twenty armoured vehicles (VBRG) in mainland France, until then "rarely used" outside the overseas territories; the decision rests with the Prime Minister, then zone prefects.[^gendinfo-centaure-vbrg-2025] Their successor, the Centaure, numbers 90 units (programme completed in 2025); according to Politis, it carries a removable 7.62 mm machine gun, which officials said was either mounted only for particular missions or "not necessarily removed".[^politis-centaure-mitrailleuses-2022] The cost is €57 million according to Politis, €60 million according to Désarmons-les.[^desarmons-matraques-2024] No source we read says it has been used in policing protests. On sound, after a video of the Bordeaux demonstration (7 November 2026), the gendarmerie replies that it was the Centaure's warning siren, subject to the same rules as other sirens, and that it owns no LRAD (acoustic cannon).[^gendinfo-vrai-faux-canons-a-son-2026] The Interior Ministry has said since June 2026 that the forces do not use LRADs in street operations, and describes a device filmed in 2021 as a megaphone with a siren-type signal: this denial covers the LRAD, not every sound device.[^senat-question-lrad-2026] Amnesty writes that "the government denies any use of acoustic weapons".[^amnesty-armes-soniques-deni-2025] We found no independent study of the effects of these sirens, and no independent source on what happened in Bordeaux.
- **Sainte-Soline: the figures and what the observatories say.** The tally published after the 25 March 2023 demonstration reports 5,015 tear-gas grenades, 89 stun grenades (désencerclement), 40 ASSD explosive devices and 81 LBD shots. In November 2025, five public-liberties observatories, relying on footage and an IGGN file revealed by Libération and Mediapart on 5 and 6 November 2025, consider that the first shots were fired without warning, that 1,375 of these grenades were GM2L, and that direct (flat-trajectory) shots were fired by at least nine of fifteen squadrons; they add that France would be the only European country to use such weapons. These are their analyses, not court findings.[^observatoires-sainte-soline-2025]
- **Municipal police in support.** In Montpellier, the Public Liberties Observatory recalls that, under a 2012 decree and a 2011 circular it cites, no public-order policing mission may be given to municipal police. On 1 October 2026 it observed municipal officers in helmets and riot shields who appeared to be acting in coordination with the BAC. In 2025 the city hall replied that its specialised team only protected its colleagues and did not take part in national police operations.[^olm-police-municipale-montpellier-2026]
- **A chilling effect.** UN experts (2019, 2026) and the CNCDH (2025) consider that restrictions, arrests and threatening rhetoric deter people from demonstrating.[^unric-experts-gilets-jaunes-2019][^cncdh-avis-espace-civique-2025][^ohchr-france-etudiants-2026] If that is you: [afraid, but I want to act](../parcours/index.md?je=peur).

## The European side: digital surveillance and access to data

Several recent European texts frame how authorities access our data and surveillance tools. For each: what the text says, what NGOs and legal professionals say about it, and what we could not verify. No source we read links them directly to demonstrations: they mostly concern criminal investigations, messaging services and the press.

- **Electronic evidence (e-Evidence, Regulation 2023/1543).** Applicable since 18 August 2026, it lets a judge or prosecutor in one EU country demand data directly from a provider in another country for criminal proceedings, within ten days (eight hours in urgent cases).[^eurlex-reglement-2023-1543] For content and traffic data, the order comes from a judge or is validated by one, for offences punishable by at least three years in prison (or certain cyber offences). The enforcing State is notified and may refuse on grounds of immunities, press freedom or a "manifest breach" of a fundamental right. The text does not require decryption. **Criticism:** EDRi (2023, on the final compromise) finds that the notification has been reduced to almost nothing, fears systematic gag orders and regrets that professional secrecy does not protect independent journalists using a mainstream messaging service.[^edri-eevidence-compromis-2023] A lawyer (2026) considers the remedy "amputated": the defence may not know an order exists.[^villagejustice-eevidence-defense-2026] **In France**, the system was not yet implemented in October 2026.[^clubjuristes-eevidence-france-2026]
- **Artificial intelligence (Regulation 2024/1689).** Since 2 February 2025, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement is banned, with three exceptions (victims, imminent or terrorist threat, suspect of a serious offence), subject to prior authorisation by a judicial or independent authority, except in urgent cases.[^eurlex-reglement-2024-1689] The text does not mention demonstrations and excludes national security from its scope. **Criticism:** in 2023, during the negotiations, EDRi warned of the risk of circumventing the rules by invoking national security;[^edri-ia-securite-nationale-2023] we have not read any analysis of actual use in France. **An example within the EU:** in June 2025, 47 organisations including the LDH wrote to the Commission about Hungarian amendments, in force since 15 April 2025, that authorise real-time facial recognition against demonstrators; they see in them the "first known violation of Article 5" of the regulation, which remains an NGO claim.[^ldh-hongrie-reconnaissance-faciale-2025]
- **Media freedom (Regulation 2024/1083).** Applicable since 8 August 2025, its Article 4 bars forcing journalists to reveal their sources or deploying spyware on their devices, except under strict exceptions (law, overriding reason of public interest, prior authorisation by a judicial or independent authority, serious offences).[^eurlex-reglement-2024-1083] **Criticism:** the European Federation of Journalists welcomes this step but says that in most States implementation is still "a long way" off.[^efj-emfa-2025] Application in France: not verified.
- **"Chat Control" (message scanning).** Regulation 2026/1881 (24 July 2026, valid until 3 April 2028) sets up a temporary derogation to combat online child sexual abuse, and states that nothing should weaken end-to-end encryption.[^eurlex-reglement-2026-1881] According to EDRi, it again allows private messages to be scanned after a few months' interruption.[^edri-chat-control-2026] In France, an op-ed by about fifty signatories including the LDH (12 February 2026) finds these measures "unsuitable and simplistic" and fears for associations and journalists if encryption disappears, while acknowledging the legitimacy of fighting child sexual abuse material.[^ldh-tribune-numerique-2026] The permanent regulation, proposed in 2022, is still under negotiation.[^oeil-csar-2022]
- **ProtectEU (access to data, encryption).** The Commission's roadmap (24 June 2025) is a plan, not a law: an impact assessment on data retention, an expert group on lawful access to encrypted data, research to decrypt after 2030.[^eurlex-com-2025-349] EDRi sees a risk of backdoors in encryption.[^edri-protecteu-2025] We found no published bill.

## Biases and observed effects

A law can be right in its aim and still affect people other than those targeted. What the sources say about the "casseurs-payeurs" bill (October 2026):

The text targets knowingly taking part in a violent group, or staying in an unlawful gathering despite warnings. The actors draw different readings from it, attributed below:

| Who | Reading of the bill |
| --- | --- |
| Government (Interior Minister) | Deter rioters and better compensate victims.[^publicsenat-casseurs-payeurs-2026] |
| CNCDH | Acknowledges the "legitimate emotion" caused by the damage, but denounces the arbitrariness of attributing harm to people with a "supposed link" and considers the measure "will very probably have a deterrent effect".[^cncdh-casseurs-payeurs-2026] |
| Amnesty International | Points to joint liability of up to €10,000 without proof of personal causation, with possible seizure of social benefits, including for damage caused by children.[^amnesty-casseurs-payeurs-2026] |
| LDH (collective op-ed republished) | Fears that "anyone protesting nearby" a perpetrator could be targeted.[^ldh-tribune-casseurs-payeurs-2026] |

- **Beyond criminal law: prefects and parents.** According to the LDH, several prefects wrote to parents of minors under investigation and passed these proceedings on to school districts, head teachers and mayors (example given: Hauts-de-Seine). The association sees a breach of investigative secrecy and of the GDPR and referred the matter to the CNIL on 27 October 2026; these facts and this legal analysis are the LDH's own, with no source cited.[^ldh-prefets-procureurs-2026]
- **A serious injury, several versions (Lens, 5 October).** A 15-year-old high-school student had his hand torn off by a grenade during a student demonstration; the IGPN is investigating (18 inquiries open as of 7 October according to the minister). The Béthune prosecutor says he cannot say "formally" whether the teenager picked the grenade up or it was thrown at him, and the prefect describes a group of 200 to 250 students throwing stones, firecrackers and mortars at police.[^franceinfo-lens-grenade-2026] The grenade model is itself disputed: GM2L according to RTL, ASSD according to a telegram from the national police directorate, EF-SON according to Le Monde (cited by a local site); that site also reports that the prosecutor reportedly said on 9 October that the shot had followed the operating procedure, which the family's lawyer disputes (not cross-checked).[^orange-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026][^brut-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026][^pandore-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026] On 7 October, the Interior Minister suspended the use of stun grenades "for the students' movement" until "light has been shed"; the measure is targeted, not a general ban.[^orange-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026][^pandore-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026]
- **What is disputed.** The exact scope of "nearby" or "knowingly" depends on the final text, which is not yet adopted: these are concerns raised by NGOs and institutions, not an observed effect.

## Arrests: the autumn 2026 figures

To our knowledge, there is no single figure for the number of high-school and university students arrested. The published tallies cover different periods and scopes, and "arrest", "police custody" and "brought before a court" are not the same stage.

- **Arrests, high-school movement (Interior Ministry).** 6,059 since 28 September according to a local paper (6 October, source not named), 6,547 before the 8 October demonstration according to media citing the Interior Ministry, and 6,629 as of 7 October according to a local news site.[^laclepublique-lyceens-justice-2026][^basta-interpellations-lyceens-2026][^brut-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026][^pandore-nunez-grenades-assourdissantes-2026] On the law-enforcement side, 715 police and gendarmes injured since 28 September according to the Interior Ministry, 761 according to another relay.
- **Police custody and follow-up (Justice Ministry, 5 October).** 5,060 police custodies since the start of the violence, 4,406 of them minors (87%) and 654 adults; 1,802 people prosecuted (1,483 minors, 319 adults), 1,376 alternative measures and 14 pre-trial detentions. The Justice Minister asked the chief prosecutors to bring, "as far as possible", the civil liability of the parents of arrested minors.[^actu17-justice-gav-lyceens-2026][^laclepublique-lyceens-justice-2026]
- **Mid-October.** 7,950 police custodies, 6,773 of them minors and 1,177 adults, 2,735 prosecutions, 2,225 alternatives to prosecution and 1,263 cases dropped, according to a tally attributed to the Justice Ministry; the exact period is not specified.[^titrespresse-gav-lyceens-2026]
- **"Cost of living" mobilisation (since 17 October).** The Justice Minister announces on 3 November 4,914 police custodies (70% adults, 30% minors), 1,820 people brought before a court and 329 incarcerations; two thirds are aged 18 to 25 and more than half had no criminal record.[^ici-darmanin-gav-2026] On 17 October, 2,397 arrests were announced (see the timeline).
- **Associations' figures.** A statement by the LDH, the FIDH and the OMCT (9 October) gives "6,629 arrests" as of 7 October and more than 5,000 police custodies, 87% of them minors: these figures match the official tallies above, but the statement does not cite its source.[^ldh-repression-lyceens] A joint statement by twelve organisations (5 October) announces "5,000 arrests" in under a week, 2,000 of them on 1 October, also without a source.[^ldh-communique-commun-jeunes-2026] A report by the Montpellier Public Liberties Observatory puts forward more than 6,000 arrests in under two weeks, 85% of them minors, without a source; it itself counts, in Montpellier, about fifteen arrests on 1 October and about ten on 2 October.[^olm-police-municipale-montpellier-2026] The LDH's 23 October op-ed mentions about 5,900 police custodies of minors, without stating where the figure comes from.[^ldh-tribune-casseurs-payeurs-2026]
- **The Defender of Rights** gives no arrest figure, but speaks of an "unprecedented" scale and has received 55 complaints, 21 of them concerning minors.[^ddd-mobilisations-lyceennes-2026]
- **Why they cannot be compared.** Cut-off dates differ, and so do the units: one outlet wrongly applies the 87% (which holds for police custodies) to arrests. Minors are about 87% in the high-school movement tallies and 30% in the one for the mobilisation since 17 October. We publish no total and found no separate figure for university students.

## The example of the health crisis (Covid-19)

A health state of emergency was in force in 2020. The decree of 31 May 2020 banned any gathering of more than ten people on the public road, demonstrations included; on 13 June 2020 the Council of State's urgent-applications judge suspended that ban for demonstrations subject to prior declaration, recalling that any restriction must be necessary, appropriate and proportionate to the public-health aim.[^ce-ordonnance-2020-06-13] On 6 July 2020 it also suspended the authorisation regime and the ban above 5,000 people for declared demonstrations,[^ce-ordonnance-2020-07-06] and on 15 January 2021 the Council of State annulled those provisions of the decree on the merits.[^ce-decision-2021-01-15] On communication, the Senate noted in December 2020 a lack of preparation, a lack of consistency in strategy and a lack of suitable communication, citing the government's wavering on wearing masks among the causes of public incomprehension; it attributes the mask shortage to the 2018 decision by the Director-General of Health not to renew the stock of surgical masks.[^senat-covid-rapport-199-synthese] These are findings of a parliamentary inquiry committee, not a judgement about intentions.

## A cultural reference: _1984_

Published in 1949, George Orwell's dystopian novel _Nineteen Eighty-Four_ imagines a society where machines called "telescreens" monitor all behaviour and where posters proclaim "Big Brother is watching you"; it deals notably with mass surveillance.[^wiki-1984-roman] A literary reference helps name a worry; it proves nothing. What this page establishes are texts, court decisions and opinions of bodies, with their sources.
